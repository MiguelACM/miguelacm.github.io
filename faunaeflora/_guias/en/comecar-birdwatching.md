## What to bring

- **Binoculars.** The only truly essential kit. An 8×42 pair is the best compromise for beginners: wide field of view, bright image and little shake. 10×42 brings birds closer but shakes more and makes flying birds harder to follow.
- **A field guide** to the birds of Europe, as a book or an app. Leafing through it at home before you go helps more than flicking pages with the bird in front of you.
- **A notebook** or your phone, to note what you saw, where and when.
- **Muted clothing** in greens, browns or greys, and walking shoes. Avoid noisy fabrics.
- Later on, if you like, a **telescope** on a tripod, useful at wetlands and estuaries where birds are far away.

## When to go

- **The first three hours after sunrise** are best: birds are active, singing and feeding. There is a second, shorter peak in the late afternoon.
- **Spring (March to June):** song, breeding plumage and the arrival of summer visitors that winter in Africa.
- **Autumn (August to October):** migration. Portugal lies on the route between Europe and Africa, and passage migrants turn up.
- **Winter:** ducks, waders and birds from northern Europe on the wetlands.
- Days with strong wind and heavy rain are usually poor.

## How to identify a bird

Before opening the guide, watch the bird for as long as you can and note, in this order:

1. **Size and shape.** Compare with birds you know: sparrow, blackbird, pigeon, gull. Note the shape of the bill and the length of the tail and legs.
2. **Behaviour.** Does it walk on the ground, creep up trunks, hover, dive? How does it fly: straight, bounding, gliding?
3. **Habitat.** Reedbed, crag, woodland, open field. Many similar species are separated by where they live.
4. **Colours and patterns.** Wing bars, bill and leg colour, head markings.
5. **Voice.** In time you'll identify more birds by ear than by eye. Apps such as Merlin help recognise songs.

> A good rule: if you can't identify a bird, note what you saw and leave it as "unidentified". That's part of it. Over time the same features start to repeat.

## Watching without disturbing

- **The bird comes first.** If it changes its behaviour because of you (stops feeding, gives alarm calls, flies off), you're too close.
- **Don't approach nests** or photograph them up close. In the breeding season, stay on paths.
- **Don't play recorded calls** to attract birds, especially in spring. It disrupts territory defence and feeding of chicks.
- **Don't share nest sites** of sensitive species such as raptors and owls on social media or public platforms.
- Respect private property and the rules of protected areas.

## Record what you see

Your sightings have scientific value. Platforms such as [eBird](https://ebird.org/) and [iNaturalist](https://www.inaturalist.org/) gather records from thousands of people and are used to study bird distribution and trends. A complete checklist, with start time, duration and every species seen, is more useful than a single record.
