A short guide to recognising the region's animals and plants and knowing when and where to look for them.

Before you go

Two worlds 20 km apart

The Sicó karst is limestone: pavements, gorges, Portuguese oak woods and over 300 caves. It is protected mainly for its bats and for animals found only in its caves. Paul de Arzila, partly in the parish of Anobra, is a reedbed marsh with 181 vertebrate species and the most reliable place to see wildlife.

Flying mammals

The cave bats

They are the main reason Sicó is protected. The massif's caves hold roosts of national importance for three cave-dwelling species. Since 2026 it is forbidden to enter caves with colonies, so watch them outside, at dusk.

{% include especies.html zona="sico" grupo="morcegos" %}
Twelve bat species of unfavourable conservation status have been recorded around the Alvaiázere wind farm, including Escalera's bat. The lesser horseshoe bat is likely in ruins and abandoned stone houses.

Birds of the hills and cliffs

Raptors over the limestone

{% include especies.html zona="sico" slugs="bubo-bubo circaetus-gallicus falco-tinnunculus buteo-buteo monticola-solitarius tachymarptis-melba" %}
Northern wheatear, corn bunting, red-legged partridgeConfirmed
Sicó plateau in spring. The corn bunting sounds like a jangling bunch of keys; the partridge calls at dawn.
Nightjars and owlsProbable
May to August, in the first hour of darkness. The European nightjar "churrs" in scrubby clearings.
Bonelli's eagle and red-billed chough are not confirmed in Sicó. A sighting would be valuable: log it on eBird or report it to ICNF, without revealing nest sites.

Paul de Arzila Nature Reserve

Herons, otters and reedbed ducks

The 1.5–2 km circuit has a boardwalk, hides and a tower. The visitor centre opens on weekdays 9:00–13:00 and 14:00–17:30. Worth a visit in any season, each bringing different species.

{% include especies.html zona="sico" slugs="ardea-purpurea ixobrychus-minutus nycticorax-nycticorax circus-aeruginosus alcedo-atthis lutra-lutra" %}
Wintering ducksConfirmed
November to February. Teal here reach 3.6% of Portugal's total.
Reed warblersConfirmed
April to July, grating songs at dawn. In autumn, migrant warblers and bluethroats pass through.

Mammals of the hills

More signs than sightings

There is no published list of Sicó's land mammals. Wild boar is confirmed; the rest are probable. Almost all are nocturnal, so the best approach is learning to read the signs they leave.

The wildcat (Endangered) is reported from Paul de Arzila, but it is easily confused with feral domestic cats. Only camera trapping and genetics can confirm it.

Amphibians and reptiles

Newts in caves, lizards on the limestone

Amphibians come out on rainy nights from autumn to late winter. Reptiles bask on sunny mornings from spring to summer. Photograph without touching.

{% include especies.html zona="sico" slugs="triturus-marmoratus salamandra-salamandra timon-lepidus lacerta-schreiberi vipera-latastei" %}
Common toadConfirmed
Crosses roads on the first rainy nights. Drive slowly near Arzila.
Tree frog and Iberian water frogProbable
A shrill chorus in the reeds from March to May; water frogs dive into the channels by day.
Midwife toadProbable
A bell-like "peep" from dry-stone walls, spring to summer.
Mediterranean pond turtleProbable
Basks on logs and channel banks; dives when it sees you.

Rivers, caves and meadows

The hidden fauna

{% include especies.html zona="sico" slugs="lampetra-planeri pseudochondrostoma-polylepis luciobarbus-bocagei iberoporus-pluto euphydryas-aurinia" %}
At least five species found nowhere else on Earth have been described from the Sicó karst: the beetle Iberoporus pluto, the rove beetle Domene lusitanica, the pseudoscorpions Roncocreagris borgesi and R. gepesi, and the woodlouse Trichoniscoides sicoensis. They live in caves around Penela and Ansião threatened by trampling, sewage and quarry dust.
Stag beetleProbable
June and July at dusk, near old Portuguese oaks.
Orchid pollinatorsProbable
March to May in the limestone meadows. Watch without trampling the orchids.

Flora

Oak woods and limestone meadows

Sicó's landscape is made of Portuguese oak woods, low scrub on limestone and dry meadows that fill with flowers and orchids in spring. This section is still being completed.
